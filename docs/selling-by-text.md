# Selling by text

What Wheelman is taught about turning an enquiry into a sale, and where it comes from. The
operational version, the one the AI reads on every suggestion, is `voice/sales-playbook.md`.
This page is the reasoning and the evidence behind it, for the people who maintain the playbook.

The situation it is written for: a used-car dealer whose customers write by text (SMS from the
dealer dashboard, Facebook Marketplace chat, WhatsApp for auction customers), a texting team in
one place and a yard team in another, and a sale that can end in several ways: an inspection at
the yard, an online video inspection for a buyer far away, a refundable holding deposit, a finance
application, delivery interstate. The replies have to move a customer to one of those ends,
honestly, in the dealer's own voice, with as few questions as possible.

## The books and frameworks, and what survives in a three-line text

- **Neil Rackham, SPIN Selling.** Too many situation questions go with failed sales: buyers find them boring. Ask only what the enquiry does not already say. A need-payoff question ("would the sliding doors make the school run easier?") lets the buyer say the benefit out loud. Small advances beat hard closes.
- **Dixon and Adamson, The Challenger Sale.** Teach one thing they did not know (what auction grade 4/B means), tailor the car to the buyer, and recommend rather than hand over a menu. Talk about money plainly.
- **Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference.** Label the fear ("buying a car you have not seen is the worrying bit"), ask "what" and "how" rather than "why", make "no" easy ("would it be a bad idea to do the video walkaround tonight?"), and after silence ask "have you gone off the Noah idea?" once, then stop.
- **Robert Cialdini, Influence.** Reciprocity (send the auction sheet unasked), commitment and consistency (a ladder of small yeses), social proof and scarcity only when literally true and checkable.
- **Phil M Jones, Exactly What to Say.** "What happens next is…" lowers fear of the process. "Before you make your mind up…" gets one last fact heard. "Most people in your spot…" only when it is true.
- **Keenan, Gap Selling.** One current-state question ("what is the current car not doing for you?"), never a full discovery by text.
- **Todd Caponi, The Transparency Sale.** Lead with the flaw and the proof. A seller who admits the dent is believed about the engine. Say who the car is not for.
- **Jeb Blount, Virtual Selling and Objections.** A neutral first beat ("fair question"), then the fact, then one ask, then silence. Never a second text an hour later.
- **Daniel Pink, To Sell Is Human.** Match their length and tone. Make the action easy: the link, the two time options.
- **Joe Girard and Carl Sewell.** Every buyer influences hundreds of people; under-promise and over-deliver; systems over talent.
- **Alex Hormozi (the CLOSER framework), Jeremy Miner (NEPQ), Josh Braun.** One connecting or problem question per thread, not a sequence; detach from the outcome; "poke the bear" with the risk they have not considered and the answer you already have (the auction sheet proves the kilometres).
- **Dixon and McKenna, The JOLT Effect.** Buyers stall from fear of choosing wrong more than from preferring to do nothing: judge the indecision, give one recommendation, limit the options, take the risk away (the refundable deposit, the warranty, their own mechanic).

## What a used-car buyer is thinking

They fear hidden faults, an unclear history, being overcharged, pressure, and a wasted trip. Trust comes from speed, a named person, specific facts, proof (photos of the flaws, a cold-start video, the auction sheet), flaws disclosed before they ask, and consistency between the ad, the text and the car. Interest dies from a slow reply, no next step, a wall of text, two questions, a generic answer, a dodged price, pushy urgency, and "??".

The stages, and the job at each one:

| Stage | Job |
|---|---|
| Is this real and available? | Confirm, and give something useful |
| Is it as described? | Proof: flaws, video, documents |
| What will it really cost me? | One honest answer on what the price includes; finance in a few minutes |
| Is it worth the trip or the risk? | The inspection booked, or the video inspection |
| Should I commit now? | The refundable deposit and what happens next |
| Did I choose well? | Reinforce the decision, keep them informed |

## The Australian rules that shape the wording

- Australian Consumer Law, sections 18 and 29: misleading conduct and false representations apply to every text, intent or not. "Only one left", "two others are asking" and "price goes up Monday" are unlawful when untrue, and the ACCC has fined retailers for fake urgency.
- Condition and history claims: the ACCC's 2025 report on second-hand cars names misrepresenting a car's history or suitability as a core harm. Nothing is said about accidents or condition beyond the records. https://www.accc.gov.au/about-us/publications/consumer-issues-in-the-sale-of-second-hand-cars
- NSW dealer guarantee: cars under 10 years old and under 160,000 km carry a 3-month or 5,000 km guarantee, and consumer guarantees cannot be excluded with "sold as is". Many imports are older or higher, so the guarantee is never promised in general. https://www.nsw.gov.au/driving-boating-and-transport/buying-and-selling-vehicles/dealer-guarantees-and-warranties
- NSW cooling-off: one business day when the sale uses dealer-linked finance. Never "no cooling-off".
- The Spam Act 2003 covers SMS and chat. Replying to an enquiry is fine; a later promotional message needs consent, so "want me to text you when a newer one lands?" is asked first. https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam

## The ten rules, as the playbook states them

1. Answer what was asked in the first sentence.
2. Say the flaw before they ask, with proof.
3. One concrete next step per reply, named as a thing that happens. Never "let us know if you have any questions".
4. At most one question, from the bank, and only one that moves the sale.
5. Short, matched to how the customer writes.
6. Recommend when they are stuck, then take the risk away.
7. Tailor the car to what they said; never guess at a buyer you do not know.
8. The deposit once interest is clear, with what it does, that it is refundable, what it needs and what happens next. Urgency lives here and nowhere else, as the true mechanism only.
9. Detach: accept a no at once, one alternative, stop.
10. Honest claims only.

## How Wheelman applies this

- `src/selling.js` works out where each customer is on the ladder (interest, proof, fit, commit, buyer) from the records: the situation of their message, website events such as a booked inspection or a started purchase, a request to hold the car, a visit we confirmed, a recorded sale. It names the one move and the one question allowed, and the AI is told these under THIS CUSTOMER'S NEXT STEP.
- The checks in `src/checks.js` refuse pressure wording and unbacked claims outright, warn on filler and on more than one question, and warn when a reply offers no next step.
- The replay script scores every draft on six points (a next step, one question at most, the length band, proof offered when it matters, no filler, no urgency outside the commit rung), so a change to the playbook can be judged before it is merged.
- Approved model replies (`voice/model-replies.md`) set the standard the AI matches; the team's real replies set the voice.
